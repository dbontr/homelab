# Nonfiction, argument, and evidence

## Map the argument

Identify the intended audience, real question, thesis or governing claim, supporting propositions, evidence for each, inferential steps, meaningful objections, and necessary qualifications. Distinguish what the author establishes from what the author merely suggests, hopes, or proposes to investigate.

A paragraph should do identifiable work: assert a point, supply evidence, explain a mechanism, draw an inference, address a limitation, or advance the argument. Do not demand a visible topic sentence or conclusion for every paragraph if the reasoning crosses paragraph boundaries naturally.

## Four independent assessments

1. **Factual accuracy:** Is the statement externally true or internally consistent? Verify when external truth matters; do not invent verification.
2. **Reasoning validity:** Does the conclusion follow, and under which assumptions? Look for missing premises, scope changes, conflation of correlation and causation, and unexamined alternatives.
3. **Evidence sufficiency:** Are sources relevant, representative, current where necessary, and strong enough for the claim's certainty?
4. **Writing quality:** Can the reader understand the mechanism, hierarchy, meaning, and point without unnecessary labor?

Do not use polished language to conceal uncertainty. Preserve real hedges, including "may," "under these assumptions," and limited-population claims. Avoid gratuitous hedging when evidence supports a stronger conclusion.

## Scientific and technical prose

Prefer mechanism over prestige language. Specify what is measured, inferred, modeled, or proposed. Distinguish proof from empirical support and necessary conditions from sufficient ones. Where relevant, give mathematical definitions, hypotheses, asymptotic regimes, physical scales, bounds, uncertainty, sample size, experimental conditions, or counterexamples instead of generic claims about significance.

Retain exact identifiers, units, equations, notation, algorithms, code symbols, and established terminology. Passive voice, noun phrases, repetition, formal structure, and standardized section headings may be correct for a discipline; do not remove them to make prose look informal. Do not replace a technical term with an approximate synonym.

## Evidence and attribution

Maintain a claim-source map for citation-heavy material: claim, supporting passage/source, nature of support, limitation, and destination citation. Verify titles, authors, dates, quotations, page ranges, links, and reported quantities against inspected material when tools permit. Mark unverified references explicitly. Never invent a citation, quotation, page number, experiment, result, or institution.

Distinguish quotation, paraphrase, synthesis, original contribution, and interpretive inference. When an originality or similarity review is requested, compare distinctive fragments with actual available sources and report possible textual overlap without asserting plagiarism or authorship from textual resemblance alone.

## Explanatory writing

Begin at the actual source of difficulty, not an inflated historical preamble. Define terms precisely where needed. Use analogy to reveal an authentic structural relation, and name its limits. Resist both unnecessary simplification and gratuitous formalism. Explain enough for the intended reader to reconstruct the reasoning; stop when the point has arrived.
